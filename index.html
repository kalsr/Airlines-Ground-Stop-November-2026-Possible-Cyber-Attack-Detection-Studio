Airlines-Ground-Stop-November-2026-Possible-Cyber-Attack-Detection-Studio

AeroSentinel is a Streamlit web app built by Randy Singh of Kalsnet (KNet) Consulting Group. It helps investigators answer one urgent question during a nationwide aviation ground stop: was it a cyber attack, or an ordinary technical or operational failure? It reads two kinds of data: minute-by-minute measurements from key FAA-style systems (NOTAM, ERAM, TFMS, SWIM and ATCSCC communications) and a security event log. You can upload your own CSV files or run one of three built-in synthetic scenarios: a cyber attack, a failed database maintenance job, or normal operations. The app compares every measurement to a "normal" baseline (the first 40% of the time window) to find unusual values, such as traffic floods, bursts of failed logins, CPU spikes and data-integrity failures. A machine-learning model called Isolation Forest also looks across all eight measurements together to flag unusual minutes. It then checks eight warning signs of an attack, each linked to a known attacker technique in MITRE ATT&CK, such as traffic floods, password guessing, a login that succeeds after many failures, logins from abroad, new admin accounts, unapproved data deletion, malware alerts and several systems failing at once. Each warning sign adds points, and signs of an ordinary failure take points away, such as approved maintenance tickets or a problem confined to a single system. The result is a 0–100 score: HIGH means a likely attack, MODERATE means investigate, LOW means a likely failure. It is written in Python with pandas and NumPy for the data and statistics, scikit-learn for the Isolation Forest model, Plotly for gauges, heat maps, timelines and charts, and ReportLab and python-docx for PDF and Word reports, with text and CSV exports too. In practice, it gives a security team a quick, evidence-based first read in minutes instead of hours, and puts network, login, integrity and malware evidence from several systems on one timeline. It also walks analysts through a standard investigation checklist, from preserving evidence to notifying the FAA, CISA and FBI, and produces reports in a common format for leadership and agencies. That can help avoid both panic over a simple IT failure and a slow response to a real attack. It works on uploaded files rather than live data, it can't block attacks or say who carried one out, its scoring weights are rough starting values that need tuning, and real FAA data isn't public, so the demo runs on made-up data.
